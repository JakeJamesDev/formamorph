## Case 1: Greeting both people (run 1)

**Player action**

> I greet the ferryman and the woman by the firepit, asking them to tell me a little about themselves.

### Previous prompt — final narration

> You limp toward the firepit, your boots heavy on the damp wood of the dock. The small woman tending the eels barely looks up from her work, poking at the smoking fish with a stick; a burn scar traces across her right cheek, and a green glass bead is braided into her hair. When you ask who they are, she just narrows her eyes. "I'm the one who catches them," she says, nodding to the river-eels. "And I'm the one who smokes them."
> 
> The broad man standing by the listing ferry doesn't move from his post. He has a brass ring in his left ear and only one arm—the right; his left sleeve is pinned up at the shoulder. He gives you a dry, sideways glance. "I run the ferry," he says shortly. "And I don't cross after dark."

### Minimal prompt — final narration

> You shuffle toward the lantern post, your boots heavy and aching. The lantern swings slightly in the damp breeze, its iron chain flaking away in pale blue scales that drift like frozen ash onto the dock. 
> 
> The ferryman stands by the mooring line. He is a broad man with only one arm; his empty left sleeve is tied neatly at the shoulder. He watches you approach with eyes as flat and grey as the river, holding the guide rope with a grip that looks like it could snap bone. 
> 
> A few paces away, a woman sits by a small, smoldering firepit dug into the gravel. She is wrapped in a heavy wool shawl that smells of brine and smoke. Deep, pale scars track across her cheek and down her neck, disappearing beneath her collar. She doesn't look up as you stop beside her, her attention fixed on a bundle of eels she is meticulously tying with twine.
> 
> "Good evening," you say, your voice soft in the heavy air. "I'm just passing through. Would you mind telling me a bit about yourselves?"
> 
> The ferryman doesn't blink. He looks at your ink-stained fingers and the gap where your ring and pinky should be, then back up to your face. He grunts once, a sound that is neither friendly nor hostile. 
> 
> "I pull the rope," he says. "I take folk across to the south bank, provided they've got coin and it's still light. I don't cross after dark."
> 
> The woman by the fire finally looks up. Her eyes are sharp, darting over your travel-worn clothes and the map case slung over your shoulder. She says nothing for a long moment, her hands continuing their work with the twine. 
> 
> "I smoke eels," she says eventually. Her voice is raspy, like gravel being dragged through water. "I sell them at the market across the river, if the tide's right and the ferryman's in a good mood."
> 
> Neither of them offers a name. They simply stand and sit in the thick silence of the gloamwater, waiting for you to state your business or move along. 
> 
> You instinctively reach for the loose end of the ferry's mooring rope, your fingers moving by habit to tie a small, tight tollow-knot for luck. The ferryman's gaze follows your hands.
